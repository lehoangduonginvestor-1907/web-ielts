If you remember only one thing about IELTS Listening and Reading, remember this: **the question and the passage almost never use the same word for the same idea.** The question writes *children*; the passage says *kids*. The question writes *cheap*; the audio says *doesn't cost much*. Test writers do this on purpose—they test whether you understand meaning, not whether you can spot matching words.

Anyone who hunts for exact words from the question runs into two problems: either they find nothing at all, or they find that exact word in the **wrong** place.

## Four common ways ideas are paraphrased

| Type | Question writes | Passage or audio says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Synonym | *The rent is* **cheap**. | *The flat is very* **affordable**. |
| Change of word form | *There was a* **rise** *in sales.* | *Sales* **rose** *sharply.* |
| Negated opposite | *The task is* **difficult**. | *The task is* **not easy**. |
| Whole-phrase explanation | *She works* **part-time**. | *She only comes in* **three mornings a week**. |

The last type is the trickiest at Band 5 because there is no single keyword to grab. You have to understand the whole phrase and ask yourself: are these two sentences saying the same thing?

## What changes and what stays the same

Not every word gets paraphrased. Knowing which words stay fixed lets you locate the right spot in the text fast.

:::rule Landmarks vs. meaning
**Landmarks** are hard to reword: proper names, numbers, years, and technical terms. They usually stay the same—use them to **find the location**.

**Meaning** is everything else: verbs, adjectives, and common nouns. These usually change—you must **read for meaning** to match them.
:::

:::steps How to apply this
1. In the question, underline one or two **landmarks** (names, numbers, years).
2. Scan for that landmark in the passage—or wait to hear it in the audio.
3. Once you reach the right spot, stop word-hunting. Read the sentence containing the landmark and the one right after it carefully.
4. Ask: is the **meaning** of the question restated here in different words?
:::

:::examples
✓ Question: In **1998** the factory [[shut down]]. Text: The plant [[closed its doors]] in **1998**. // 1998 is the landmark and stays the same. *shut down* becomes *closed its doors*.
✓ Question: **Dr Lee** thinks the results are [[unreliable]]. Text: **Lee** warns that the figures [[cannot be trusted]]. // The name is the landmark. The adjective is replaced by a whole clause.
✗ Question: The museum is [[free]] for students. Text: Students are [[free]] to take photos in the museum. // Exact word match (*free*), completely different meaning: free of charge vs. allowed to.
:::

## The word-match trap

That last example is the most common trap across both skills. Test writers deliberately plant a word that looks **identical** to the question in a wrong option, knowing that rushed test-takers will pick by sight or sound.

:::trap Be suspicious of exact word matches
When an option repeats a phrase word-for-word from the passage or audio, re-read the whole sentence before choosing it. The right answer is usually **reworded**. An exact-match option often fails on a small detail: wrong person, wrong timeframe, or a hidden negation.
:::

:::check
Passage: *"Most villagers had never left the valley, and few could read."*

Which sentence below expresses the same meaning?
- [ ] Few villagers lived in the valley.
- [x] The majority of local people were illiterate.
- [ ] Most villagers wanted to leave the valley.
> *few could read* is paraphrased as *the majority were illiterate*. The other two options recycle *few*, *villagers*, *valley*, and *leave* while changing the meaning completely.
:::

:::check
In a Listening recording, the speaker says: *"We used to open on Sundays, but we stopped that last year."*

Which note is accurate?
- [ ] Open on Sundays
- [x] Closed on Sundays
- [ ] Opened last year
> *used to ... but we stopped* means it happened in the past, not now. You clearly hear *open on Sundays*, but the confirmed current state is **closed** on Sundays.
:::

## How to build this habit

After every practice set in SealIELTS, each explanation includes a **Paraphrase** section showing what the question said versus what the text or audio said. Don't skip it. For every mistake, ask yourself: *Did I miss this because I didn't know these two expressions meant the same thing, or because I hadn't found the right spot?* If it's the first reason, save that pair to your flashcards.

After a few dozen exercises, you will notice test writers recycling the same families over and over: *increase / go up / rise*, *problem / difficulty / challenge*, *important / essential / vital / key*. That synonym bank raises your band score more than any trick ever will.
