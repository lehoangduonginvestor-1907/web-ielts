Summary completion with a word list looks similar to standard gap-fill, except it includes a box of lettered words (A, B, C...) below the summary. The word list **usually contains more words than there are gaps**.

The biggest difference to keep in mind: **the right word in the box is usually a synonym of a word in the passage**, and rarely matches the passage word-for-word.

## Find the source section before looking at the word list

A summary usually covers **only part of the passage** (two or three consecutive paragraphs), not necessarily the whole text. Your first job is to locate the matching section.

:::steps How to tackle Summary completion with a word list
1. Skim the summary to grasp the topic, underlining one or two **anchors** (proper nouns, years, technical terms) in the first or last sentence.
2. Use those anchors to locate the corresponding **section of the passage**.
3. Read each sentence with a gap and **filter the word list by grammar first** (does the gap need a singular noun, plural noun, past-tense verb, or adjective?).
4. Read the matching part of the passage carefully to find the **missing idea**.
5. Match that idea against the remaining options after your grammar filter, and pick the closest synonym.
:::

## Filter by grammar to cut the list in half

Even if your range of synonyms is still developing, filtering by grammar eliminates half the wrong options in seconds.

| Context around the gap | Required word form | Words to rule out immediately |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "a ____ system" | Adjective (or compound noun) starting with a consonant sound | Verbs, plural nouns, words starting with a vowel sound |
| "many ____" | Plural countable noun | Singular nouns, adjectives, verbs |
| "helped to ____" | Base verb (without *to*) | Nouns, adjectives, *-ed* or *-ing* verbs |
| "was ____ by" | Past participle (*-ed* / 3rd form) or adjective | Nouns, base verbs |

:::examples
✓ Passage: *"The bridge remained sturdy for a century."* Summary: "The structure stayed ____ for a hundred years." Word list: A. *fragile*, B. *strong*, C. *construction*, D. *build*. → Rule out C (noun) and D (verb). Between A and B, *sturdy* matches *strong*, whereas *fragile* means the opposite. // Filter by grammar first, then check meaning.
✗ Passage: *"Local residents opposed the new railway."* Summary: "The project faced strong ____ from villagers." Seeing *railway* in the box and picking it because it appears in the passage. // Word-matching trap: after *strong*, you need a noun describing attitude (*opposition*), not *strong railway*.
:::

:::trap Picking a word copied straight from the passage
If a word in the list repeats an exact word from that part of the passage, be cautious. Test writers often insert passage words as distractors; plug them into the summary and you will notice they have the **wrong word form** or the **wrong meaning**.
:::

:::check
Passage: *"Because winter storms frequently snapped the wooden poles, engineers replaced them with buried cables that could survive freezing winds."*

Summary: "To prevent damage from severe weather, workers installed underground cables instead of exposed poles, making the network much more ____."
Word list:
A. *vulnerable*
B. *durable*
C. *weather*
D. *replaced*

Which option fits the gap?
- [ ] A. *vulnerable*
- [x] B. *durable*
- [ ] D. *replaced*
> After *more*, you need an adjective (ruling out the noun *weather*). The passage says underground cables could *survive freezing winds* unlike wooden poles that snapped, meaning the network became stronger (*durable*), the opposite of *vulnerable*. *Replaced* does not make sense at the end of the sentence.
:::

:::check
Passage: *"At first, city libraries refused to lend out sheet music, fearing the pages would be torn."*

Summary: "Initially, libraries did not ____ musicians to borrow printed scores because staff worried about damage."
Word list:
A. *allow*
B. *refuse*
C. *permission*
D. *torn*

What is the correct answer?
- [x] A. *allow*
- [ ] B. *refuse*
- [ ] C. *permission*
> After *did not*, you must use a base verb (ruling out *permission* and *torn*). Because the summary already has the negative *did not*, *refused to lend* means *did not allow ... to borrow*. Picking *refuse* creates a double negative with the wrong meaning.
:::

## When two words both fit grammatically and look close in meaning

Check the **subject** and the **direction of meaning (positive or negative)** in the summary sentence. Two adjectives can both fit grammatically, while one is positive (such as *affordable*, *practical*) and the other is negative (such as *costly*, *inconvenient*). Look back at the original sentence in the passage to check the tone, and you will tell them apart immediately.
