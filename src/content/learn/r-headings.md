Matching headings gives you a list of headings marked with Roman numerals (i, ii, iii...) and asks you to pick the best title for each paragraph (A, B, C...). The catch is that **there are always more headings than paragraphs**, leaving extra distractors in the list.

Your goal is to identify the **main idea of the entire paragraph**, not a single minor detail tucked inside one sentence.

## Where to read inside each paragraph

Academic paragraphs usually put their main point near the opening or closing, then use the middle sentences to explain or give examples. That means you don't need to read every word line by line right away.

:::steps How to choose a heading for each paragraph
1. Read the Roman-numeral headings first and underline 1 or 2 core words in each.
2. Tackle the **shortest or clearest paragraphs first**—you do not have to work strictly from A to the end.
3. For each paragraph, read the **first two sentences** and the **final sentence** closely, then skim the middle to confirm the whole paragraph revolves around that idea.
4. Summarise the paragraph in a short phrase of your own, then compare it to the list of headings.
5. Once you are confident in a heading, **cross it off** to narrow the choices for the remaining paragraphs.
:::

## Detail traps and word-match traps

:::trap Identical words are the easiest way to get fooled
Test writers love pulling a vivid word from a middle example sentence and dropping it into a wrong heading. A heading that repeats exact words from the paragraph usually refers to **one tiny detail**, whereas the right heading uses **synonyms or a broader summary** covering the whole paragraph.
:::

:::examples
✓ A paragraph describes rotting timber bridges, cracked bricks, and 50 years without maintenance funding. Right heading: *Reasons for the structure's decline*. // Sums up the entire paragraph with *decline* instead of repeating *wood* or *brick*.
✗ A paragraph mentions one sentence—*"In 1920, a storm damaged the roof."*—then spends the rest explaining how residents raised funds for repairs. Picking *Extreme weather in the 1920s*. // Detail trap: the storm is just the opening hook; the main idea is fundraising and repair.
:::

| Signs of a right heading | Signs of a trap heading |
| --- | --- |
| Covers the focus of most of the paragraph | Matches only one example or statistic |
| Uses broader summary words (*benefits, challenges, early history*) | Repeats a specific phrase verbatim from the text |
| Aligns with the opening topic and closing conclusion | Contradicts the paragraph or stretches beyond what it says |

:::check
Paragraph: *"For decades, city planners assumed that widening roads would ease traffic jams. However, recent surveys from three European cities show the opposite: extra lanes quickly attract more private cars, leaving journey times unchanged within two years. As a result, several councils have begun turning car lanes into cycle paths instead."*

Which heading fits the whole paragraph best?
- [ ] *A survey of European cycle paths*
- [x] *Why adding more road space fails to reduce congestion*
- [ ] *How private cars replaced public transport*
> Sentence 1 gives the old assumption (*widening roads would ease traffic jams*), sentence 2 explains why it fails (*attract more private cars*), and sentence 3 notes the shift to cycle paths. *Cycle paths* is only the closing consequence; the thread running through the whole paragraph is that widening roads does not ease congestion.
:::

:::check
Paragraph: *"Unlike regular coffee beans, wild forest beans contain almost no caffeine. Scientists in Madagascar found that local birds avoid eating them, yet the plants still survive because small mammals carry the seeds across the valley floor."*

Which heading is best?
- [ ] *Health effects of caffeine on birds*
- [ ] *Reasons why forest beans are disappearing*
- [x] *Unusual features and seed spread of a wild plant*
> The paragraph covers two core points: an unusual trait (*almost no caffeine*) and how the plant disperses (*small mammals carry the seeds*). The first wrong option recycles *caffeine* and *birds* with the wrong meaning; the second claims the plants are disappearing, contradicting *still survive*.
:::

## When two headings look almost identical

If you are nearly done and stuck between two very similar headings for one paragraph, don't guess blindly. Put the two headings side by side, pinpoint the **single key difference** between them (for example *past* vs. *future*, or *causes* vs. *solutions*), and check which side most of the paragraph supports. Doing the easiest paragraphs first always leaves fewer distractors to untangle.
