This question type gives you a series of statements and asks whether each is **TRUE**, **FALSE**, or **NOT GIVEN** according to the passage. *YES / NO / NOT GIVEN* works the exact same way, except it asks about the **writer's views** rather than factual information.

Around Band 5–6, this is where candidates lose the most marks—almost always for one reason: confusing FALSE with NOT GIVEN.

## What the three answers mean

:::rule Definitions to memorise
- **TRUE**: the passage states the **same idea** as the statement (usually in different words).
- **FALSE**: the passage says the **opposite**. You can point to a sentence in the text that contradicts the statement.
- **NOT GIVEN**: the passage **does not say** whether it is true or false. It may mention the same topic, but it is missing the specific detail the statement claims.
:::

Use this test to separate FALSE from NOT GIVEN: **"Can I point to a sentence in the passage that contradicts it?"** If yes, it is FALSE. If not—even after reading the right section carefully—it is NOT GIVEN.

:::examples
✓ Text: *The bridge was completed in 1932.* Statement: *The bridge was finished in the early 1930s.* → **TRUE** // *completed in 1932* and *finished in the early 1930s* express the same idea.
✓ Text: *The bridge was completed in 1932.* Statement: *The bridge was finished before 1930.* → **FALSE** // 1932 directly contradicts "before 1930". You can point to the contradicting line.
✓ Text: *The bridge was completed in 1932.* Statement: *The bridge was the longest in the country.* → **NOT GIVEN** // The text mentions the bridge, but never compares its length with any other bridge.
:::

## Step-by-step approach

:::steps
1. Read the statement and underline the **anchors** (names, numbers, years) and the **claim** (what is being stated about them).
2. Use the anchors to find the right spot in the passage. Questions in this task **follow the order of the text**, so Q3 comes after Q2.
3. Read two or three sentences around that spot carefully. Compare **every part** of the statement against the text, not just the general gist.
4. Decide: same idea = TRUE. Direct contradiction = FALSE. Right location, missing detail = NOT GIVEN.
5. If you still cannot find any mention after about a minute, lean toward NOT GIVEN and move on.
:::

## Small words that flip the answer

A statement is often mostly accurate and only goes wrong on one tiny word. Train your eye to stop at these words:

| Word type | Examples | Common trap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quantifiers | *all, most, some, few, only* | Text says *some*, statement says *all* → FALSE |
| Certainty | *will, may, might, probably* | Text says *may*, statement says *will* → no longer TRUE |
| Frequency | *always, usually, sometimes, never* | Text says *often*, statement says *always* → FALSE |
| Comparisons | *more than, the first, the largest* | Text makes no comparison → NOT GIVEN |
| Cause & effect | *because, led to, as a result of* | Text mentions two events without linking them as cause and effect → NOT GIVEN |

:::trap Don't rely on outside knowledge
A statement might be true in real life, yet never appear in the passage. In that case, the answer is still NOT GIVEN. You are answering "Does the passage say this?", not "Is this true in the real world?"
:::

:::trap Comparisons are the easiest trap to fall into
Statement: *Method A is cheaper than Method B.* The passage explains how cheap Method A is, then describes Method B in another paragraph, without **comparing the two**. The answer is NOT GIVEN. To be TRUE or FALSE, the passage must actually make a comparison.
:::

:::check
Passage: *"The first bicycles had no pedals; riders pushed themselves along with their feet. Pedals were added in the 1860s by a French blacksmith."*

Statement: *Early bicycles were difficult to ride.*
- [ ] TRUE
- [ ] FALSE
- [x] NOT GIVEN
> The text tells us early bicycles had no pedals and riders pushed with their feet, but it never says whether this was **easy or difficult**. Assuming it was hard is your own inference, not the text's.
:::

:::check
Same passage. Statement: *Pedals were part of the original bicycle design.*
- [ ] TRUE
- [x] FALSE
- [ ] NOT GIVEN
> The passage explicitly states the first bicycles had **no** pedals and pedals were only added in the 1860s. You can point directly to the contradicting sentence, so it is FALSE.
:::

:::check
Passage: *"Some researchers believe the drawings were made by children."*

Statement: *Researchers agree that children made the drawings.*
- [ ] TRUE
- [x] FALSE
- [ ] NOT GIVEN
> *Some researchers believe* is very different from *researchers agree* (consensus). The quantifier *some* makes the statement contradict the passage.
:::

## Final checks before moving to the next task

- A set of questions usually includes **all three** answer types. If you have zero NOT GIVEN answers—or far too many—double-check your choices.
- In the app, you click buttons so you cannot misspell an answer. On a paper test, writing *TRUE* when the instructions ask for *YES* costs you the mark, so always check which set is requested.
- After finishing a practice test, read the explanations even for the NOT GIVEN questions you got right. Seeing exactly where the text stops and which detail is missing is the fastest way to build an instinct for NOT GIVEN.
