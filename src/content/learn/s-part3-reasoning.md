Speaking Part 3 lasts 4–5 minutes and features broader, more abstract questions linked to your Part 2 topic. This is where candidates break into **Band 6.5 and above** instead of plateauing in the middle bands, because the examiner is assessing your ability to discuss wider issues rather than tell personal stories.

:::rule The golden rule of Part 3: Step outside "I"
- In Parts 1 and 2, you talk about *I / my family*. In Part 3, zoom out to **people in general, younger generations, governments, and companies**.
- There is no right or wrong opinion. The examiner grades how well you **explain ideas, weigh both sides, and give broad examples**.
:::

## The O-R-E-C framework for thoughtful answers

:::steps
1. **O (Opinion / Nuanced Answer)**: Avoid rigid black-and-white replies. Use balanced openings (*Well, it largely depends on..., While X is true, I'd argue that...*).
2. **R (Reason)**: Give 1–2 core reasons from a social, economic, or psychological angle.
3. **E (Example / Contrast)**: Offer a common real-world example or compare **the past vs today**, or **younger vs older generations**.
4. **C (Condition / Counterpoint)**: Add a brief qualification or contrast (*Having said that, if governments fail to...*) to give your answer real depth.
:::

## Turning abstract questions into clear spoken answers

:::examples
✓ **Examiner**: *Why do some people enjoy risky outdoor activities?* → *"I think it boils down to a sense of achievement and escaping routine. Nowadays, most urban workers spend hours staring at screens, so challenging themselves in nature offers a rare adrenaline rush. That said, older generations might view those activities as unnecessary, preferring safer ways to unwind."* // Balances two perspectives, compares age groups, and uses natural phrasing (*boils down to, urban workers, adrenaline rush*).
✗ **Examiner**: *Why do some people enjoy risky outdoor activities?* → *"For example, my brother really likes mountain climbing because he wants to take nice photos."* // Falls back on a single personal anecdote without analysing the broader question about *"some people"*.
:::

:::trap Going silent when a question feels unfamiliar
If the examiner asks about a social issue you have never thought about, don't freeze. Acknowledge it naturally and reason from what you know: *"That's quite a tricky issue and I haven't looked closely at the statistics, but from what I've observed in my city..."*.
:::

## Tools for building multi-sided arguments

| Angle | Useful spoken frame | Best suited for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Split by group | *It depends heavily on age / income level...* | General opinion questions (*Do people prefer...?*) |
| Compare over time | *Compared to a decade ago, people now tend to...* | Questions about trends and change |
| Hypothetical (Condition) | *If companies invested more in X, employees would...* | Solutions or future predictions |
| Concession | *Admittedly, X brings benefits; however, Y remains a concern.* | Pros-and-cons social issues |
