Reading multiple choice questions usually come with four wordy options (A, B, C, D). If you read all four options before locating the right section of the passage, you cram four competing stories into your head and make it easy for distractors to mislead you.

The good news: Reading multiple choice questions **follow the order of the passage**, except for an occasional final question asking about the writer's overall purpose or the best title.

## Answer in your own words first, then check A–D

:::steps How to tackle Reading multiple choice
1. Read **only the question stem**—ignore options A, B, C, and D for now. Underline landmarks or the core question.
2. Use those landmarks (plus the order from the previous question) to find the right **paragraph** and the 2 or 3 relevant sentences.
3. Read those sentences closely and **form your own short answer** in a few words.
4. Now read all 4 options, select the one that best matches your answer, and verify why the other 3 are wrong.
:::

:::trap Reading all four options first invites word-matching traps
Every wrong option borrows real words from that exact paragraph. Reading the options beforehand primes your eyes to hunt for those words and makes you pick a false statement simply because its vocabulary appears in the text.
:::

## Four classic distractor patterns

When torn between two options, check whether one falls into any of these four traps:

| Distractor type | How to spot it | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| True but **off-topic** | Factually stated in the paragraph, yet doesn't answer the question | Question asks *Why did he resign?*; option mentions he moved to London afterward |
| **Too extreme** | Uses absolutes like *all, always, only, impossible* | Passage says *most visitors*; option says *all visitors* |
| **Wrong attribution** | Belongs to someone else, not the writer or named person | Question asks the writer's view; option quotes what *"critics argue"* |
| **Opposite meaning** | Flips rise to fall, or negative to positive | Passage says *rarely succeeded*; option says *frequently worked* |

:::examples
✓ Stem: *What surprised Dr. Alvarez about the cave paintings?* Passage: *"Alvarez expected simple animal outlines, so seeing detailed maps of local rivers left her astonished."* → Own answer: *surprised by river maps*. Pick the option *contained accurate geographical features*. // Answering first sidesteps the *animal outlines* trap.
✗ Picking *they showed unfamiliar animals* just because *animal* appears in the same sentence. // *animal outlines* was what she *expected*, not what astonished her.
:::

## Final questions on purpose or best title

At the end of a long passage, you may get *"What is the writer's main purpose?"* or *"Which is the best title?"* Don't re-read the whole text. Review the **opening paragraph**, **closing paragraph**, and the **overall arc** you already know. The right option must cover the **entire passage**, not just one paragraph.

:::check
Paragraph: *"Many builders avoid bamboo because they assume it rots quickly in damp climates. In fact, when treated with natural salt solutions, bamboo frames can last over forty years. The main barrier today is not durability, however, but a shortage of skilled carpenters trained to join the poles without metal nails."*

Question: *According to the writer, what currently limits the wider use of bamboo in construction?*
- [ ] It decays rapidly in wet weather.
- [x] Too few workers know how to assemble it properly.
- [ ] Natural salt treatments are too expensive.
> *currently limits* paraphrases *The main barrier today*. *Decays rapidly* is a false assumption (*they assume*, disproved by *In fact*). The cost of salt treatments is never mentioned. The real limit is at the end: a *shortage of skilled carpenters trained to join the poles*.
:::

:::check
Paragraph: *"Local shopkeepers feared the new pedestrian-only street would cut their customer numbers. Six months after cars were banned, foot traffic actually rose by twenty percent, though some hardware stores reported fewer bulk purchases because shoppers could no longer park outside."*

Question: *What happened after private cars were excluded from the street?*
- [ ] All local shops saw a drop in sales.
- [ ] Shopkeepers asked for cars to be allowed back.
- [x] More people walked along the street than before.
> Option A is too extreme (*All* is wrong since *foot traffic rose*, and only *some hardware stores* saw fewer bulk purchases). Option B is not mentioned. *Foot traffic actually rose by twenty percent* matches *More people walked along the street than before*.
:::

## When stuck between two options

Don't vaguely re-read the whole paragraph. Put the two remaining options side by side, isolate the **single sharpest difference** between them, and return to the exact evidence sentence to see what the text really states. If you pass 90 seconds, eliminate the weakest choice, pick the stronger one, and keep moving.
